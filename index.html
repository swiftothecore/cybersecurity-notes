Module 1 · Lesson 1

Core Security Principles

The foundational model every other security concept builds on: what we're protecting, and how we control who can touch it.

Core Model

◉Confidentiality

  • Ensures information is only accessible to authorised people, systems, or processes
  • Enforced through encryption, access controls, and classification levels
  • Breach = an unauthorised party reads or copies data they shouldn't
scenario.log
unencrypted database backup left on a public S3 bucket
→ anyone with the URL can read every customer record. Confidentiality failed even though nothing was "hacked."
Core Model

◈Integrity

  • Ensures data stays accurate and unaltered unless changed by an authorised action
  • Enforced through hashing, digital signatures, checksums, and version control
  • Breach = data is modified, corrupted, or forged without detection
Core Model

◎Availability

  • Ensures systems and data are accessible when needed by authorised users
  • Enforced through redundancy, backups, load balancing, and DDoS protection
  • Breach = legitimate users can't reach the system — e.g. a ransomware outage or a DoS attack
Access Control

▣Authentication

Proving who you are — passwords, biometrics, hardware tokens, or a combination (MFA).

Access Control

▤Authorisation

Defining what you're allowed to do once identity is confirmed — enforced through permissions, roles, and the principle of least privilege.

Access Control

▥Accounting

Recording what actually happened — audit logs, timestamps, and session records used for review and incident response.

Term

Asset

Anything of value worth protecting — data, hardware, software, or reputation.

Term

Vulnerability

A weakness that could be exploited to cause harm.

Term

Threat

Anything with the potential to exploit a vulnerability.

Term

Exploit

The specific method or code used to take advantage of a vulnerability.

Term

Risk

The likelihood of a threat exploiting a vulnerability, multiplied by the impact if it does.

Threat Landscape

Who's actually behind the attacks, and what makes a system worth attacking in the first place.

Threat Actor

▲Script Kiddieslow capability

Use pre-built tools without deep technical understanding. Often opportunistic rather than targeted.

Threat Actor

▲Hacktivistsmedium capability

Motivated by political or social causes. Favour defacement, leaks, and denial-of-service to make a statement.

Threat Actor

▲Organised Crimehigh capability

Financially motivated. Behind most ransomware and large-scale data-theft-for-profit operations.

Threat Actor

▲Nation-State Actorscritical capability

Well-funded, patient, and highly capable. Pursue espionage, sabotage, or long-term strategic access — Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs).

Threat Actor

▲Insider Threatsmedium capability

Current or former employees/contractors with legitimate access, acting maliciously or carelessly.

Concept

◐Attack Surface

The sum of every point where an unauthorised user could try to enter or extract data from a system — every open port, form field, API endpoint, and user account.

↑ increases exposure
  • Unpatched software and outdated systems
  • Excessive open ports and services
  • Overly broad user permissions
  • Shadow IT — unmanaged, unapproved tools
↓ reduces exposure
  • Regular patching and update cycles
  • Closing unused ports and services
  • Principle of least privilege
  • Asset inventory and approved software lists

Malware Types

Malicious software, grouped by how it spreads and what it does once it's in.

Malware

✱Virus

  • Attaches itself to a legitimate file or program
  • Needs a human action to spread — opening the infected file
  • Payload triggers once executed: corruption, theft, or further spreading
Malware

✱Worm

  • Self-replicating — spreads across networks with no user interaction required
  • Exploits vulnerabilities directly to jump between systems
  • Can cripple networks purely through the volume of traffic it generates
⚠ indicators
  • Unexplained slow performance or crashes
  • Unusual outbound network traffic
  • Files that appear, disappear, or change size
✓ mitigations
  • Keep endpoint protection and definitions current
  • Patch known vulnerabilities promptly
  • Network segmentation to contain spread
Malware

✱Ransomwarecritical impact

  • Encrypts a victim's files and demands payment for the decryption key
  • Often paired with double extortion — data is stolen before encryption, and leaked if payment is refused
  • Common entry points: phishing emails and exposed remote-access services
readme_recover.txt
all your files have been encrypted
pay 2.4 BTC within 72 hours or the price doubles — a typical ransom-note pattern, designed to pressure a fast, unverified decision.
⚠ indicators
  • Files renamed with unfamiliar extensions
  • A ransom note appearing on the desktop
  • Sudden inability to open common file types
✓ mitigations
  • Regular, offline/immutable backups
  • Disable unnecessary remote access (e.g. RDP)
  • Email filtering and user awareness training
Malware

✱Trojan

Disguises itself as legitimate software to trick the user into installing it. Unlike a virus, it doesn't self-replicate — it relies entirely on deception to get in.

Malware

✱Spyware

Runs quietly in the background, collecting information — keystrokes, browsing habits, credentials — and sending it back to an attacker.

Phishing & Network Attacks

Attacks aimed at people and at the connections between systems, rather than the systems themselves.

Social Engineering

✉Phishing

  • Fraudulent messages impersonating a trusted source to trick a victim into acting
  • Spear phishing — targeted at a specific individual using researched personal details
  • Whaling — spear phishing aimed at senior executives
  • Smishing / Vishing — the same technique via SMS or phone call
inbox — 1 new message
"URGENT: your account will be suspended — verify now"
a mismatched sender domain and a link that doesn't match the display text are the two fastest tells.
Network Attack

⇶Denial of Service

Floods a system with traffic or requests until it can no longer serve legitimate users — a direct attack on availability.

Network Attack

⇶Distributed Denial of Service

The same attack, launched from a botnet of many compromised devices at once — far harder to block by source alone.

Network Attack

⇄Man-in-the-Middle (MITM)

  • Attacker secretly positions themselves between two parties in a conversation
  • Can read, and in some cases alter, traffic without either side knowing
  • Common on unsecured public Wi-Fi, or via ARP/DNS spoofing
⚠ indicators
  • Unexpected certificate warnings
  • URLs that don't match the expected domain
✓ mitigations
  • Enforce HTTPS/TLS everywhere
  • Avoid sensitive activity on untrusted networks; use a VPN
